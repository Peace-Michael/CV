Diabetes Risk Prediction & Clustering

Applying Logistic Regression and K-Means clustering on the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset to predict diabetes risk and uncover clinically meaningful patient subgroups.

Knowledge Representation in Artificial Intelligence

Knowledge Representation (KR) forms the foundation of artificial intelligence (AI) systems, as it provides formal mechanisms through which machines code, store, and combine information to support useful reasoning and decision making (Russell and Norvig, 2021). Effectiveness of AI systems depends on how knowledge or data input is structured, as representation methods directly influence computational and reasoning capabilities, and the ability to predict direction of uncertainty (Hitsler et al., 2021). In modern applications, from user support to autonomous systems, selection of appropriate KR techniques determines how systems adapt to dynamic environments and complex real-world scenarios.

Semantic Networks and Ontologies

Semantic networks outline knowledge as graphs comprising of nodes, which refer to concepts and labeled edges showing the type of relationships, enabling intuitive visualisation of associative structures (Sowa, 2014). Modern extensions of this approach, particularly ontologies such as the Web Ontology Language (OWL), provide formal semantics and enhanced interpretation for domain modeling (Grüninger and Fox, 2022). In healthcare settings, ontologies like the Human Phenotype Ontology structure medical knowledge hierarchically, enabling which enables automated reasoning on topics like disease classifications and treatment protocols (Bodenreider and Stevens, 2006; Schriml et al., 2020).

Challenge: Semantic networks and ontologies face critical limitations in dynamic environments. Their static graph structures struggle to represent season or time-based dynamics and probabilistic relationships (Ghanadbashi, and Golpayegani, 2024).

Logic-Based Approaches

Logic-based representations, including first-order logic and description logics, provide precise, formal semantics enabling deductive reasoning (Baader et al., 2017). It involves the use of production rule systems like "IF glucose ≥126 mg/dL AND fasting THEN diabetes_diagnosis", which offer transparency and align with clinical practice guidelines (Jackson, 2019).

Frame-Based Representations

Frames organise knowledge into structured objects with slots for attributes, defaults, and procedural attachments (Brachman and Levesque, 2004). While computationally efficient and cognitively intuitive, frames inherit semantic networks' inability to handle uncertainty and temporal reasoning (Russell and Norvig, 2021).

Practical Implementation

Early detection of diabetes and the classification of individuals into clinically meaningful subgroups are foundational to effective diabetes management. In this project, Logistic Regression was employed as supervised learning method to predict occurrence of diabetes based on commonly collected clinical features from the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset.

Dataset Overview

The Pima Indians Diabetes dataset comprises 768 patient records with 8 clinical and demographic features and a binary target variable indicating diabetes status (0 = No Diabetes; 1 = Diabetes). The predictor variables include: number of pregnancies, plasma glucose concentration, diastolic blood pressure, triceps skinfold thickness, serum insulin levels, body mass index (BMI), age, and the diabetes pedigree function.

Figure 1: Outcome distribution (258 diabetes, 500 non-diabetes)

Data Quality Assessment

Initial assessment revealed several features contain sero values indicative of missing measurements: Insulin (48.7%), Skin Thickness (29.6%), Blood Pressure (4.6%), and Glucose (0.65%). Median imputation was applied to handle missing values.

Figure 2: Feature distribution by outcome

Model Training

Hyperparameter optimisation was conducted using grid search with stratified five-fold cross-validation. The optimal model used C=0.01, L2 regularisation, and liblinear solver, achieving a cross-validated F1-score of 0.6126.

K-Means Clustering

K-Means was used for patient segmentation, with four clusters selected based on Elbow Method and silhouette analysis.
