Data Visualization – World Happiness Report

Exploring global happiness through multi-dimensional data visualization using Python, pandas, seaborn, and matplotlib on the World Happiness Report dataset (2015–2019).

Tools Python, pandas, matplotlib, seaborn, Plotly

Source Kaggle (unsdsn/world-happiness)

1. Introduction

Data visualization helps to reveal analytical findings from already processed data. Raw data types in isolation provide limited understanding; therefore, when arranged data is placed in context and given form with appropriate visualization techniques, they tend to provide revelations to underlying relationships, trends and patterns within the data.

The dataset selected for this study is the World Happiness Report, compiled annually by the Sustainable Development Solutions Network. The dataset is derived from the Gallup World Poll, polling respondents from over 150 countries using the Cantril Ladder scale (0–10). The dataset includes six explanatory factors: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption.

Main Theme: Investigating factors that strongly influence countries' happiness scores and how these factors evolved across regions between 2015 and 2019.

2. Dataset Description

The dataset comprises five annual CSV files (2015–2019). After merging, the combined dataset contained 782 country-year observations spanning 166 unique countries and 10 regions.

3. Data Cleaning and Pre-Processing

Data quality assessment and preprocessing were conducted prior to the analysis. Column name discrepancies across years required harmonization using a yearly mapping dictionary. The combined dataset contained 782 rows and 11 columns.

  • Missing values: Only one missing attribute (UAE, 2018, Perceptions of Corruption) – imputed with median value.
  • Duplicates: No duplicate records found.
  • Range: Happiness scores ranged from 2.69 (Burundi, 2016) to 7.77 (Finland, 2019).
  • Standardization: Column names converted to snake_case (e.g., "Happiness Score" → "Happiness_Score").

The processed dataset comprised 782 country-year observations across 166 countries, 10 regions, and 5 years.

4. Visualisation and Analysis

Data storytelling involves guiding viewers through a logical progression of insights. This study progressively moved from descriptive analysis to regional comparisons, variable associations, trends, and multi-dimensional relationships.

4.1 Global Happiness Distribution

Figure 1: Histogram with KDE overlay of all 782 country-year Happiness Scores (2015–2019)

Findings: The distribution is approximately symmetric with a mean of 5.38 and median of 5.32. Most countries fall between 4.0 and 7.0, with a global standard deviation of 1.13.

4.2 Average Happiness Score by Region

Figure 2: Average Happiness Score by Region (2015–2019)

Findings: Australia & New Zealand lead at 7.29; Sub-Saharan Africa records the lowest at 4.19. The 3.1-point gap signals deep structural inequality across regions.

4.3 Top 10 and Bottom 10 Countries (2019)

Figure 3: Horizontal bar charts comparing the ten happiest and ten least happy countries in 2019

Findings: Finland ranked highest (7.77), followed by Denmark, Norway, Iceland, and the Netherlands. The gap between the highest and lowest was 4.92 points—nearly half the entire ladder scale.

4.4 Pearson Correlation Heatmap

Figure 4: Pearson correlation matrix showing pairwise linear relationships

Findings: GDP per capita (r=0.80) is the strongest predictor, followed by healthy life expectancy (r=0.76), social support (r=0.74), and freedom (r=0.65). Generosity shows the weakest relationship (r=0.14).

4.5 GDP Contribution vs Happiness Score

Figure 5: Scatter plot of GDP contribution score against Happiness Score (2019)

Findings: Clear positive association between GDP and happiness. Western European countries cluster in the upper right, Sub-Saharan African in the lower left. Costa Rica sits above the trend line, while some Middle Eastern countries sit below.

4.6 Pairplot: Happiness Score and Top Predictors

Figure 6: Seaborn pairplot showing pairwise scatterplots and KDE diagonals for Happiness Score, GDP per Capita, Social Support, and Healthy Life Expectancy (2019)

Findings: Clear regional clustering—Western European countries consistently occupy the upper end of all four variables. The Social Support panel shows a bimodal distribution, indicating intra-region heterogeneity.

4.7 Trends Over Time by Region

Figure 7: Average Happiness Score by region across 2015–2019

Findings: Regional rankings remained remarkably stable throughout the five-year period, with changes typically within ±0.2 points. Southern Asia recorded the most noticeable decline.

4.8 Distribution Shape by Region

Figure 8: Violin plots showing the full distribution shape of Happiness Scores by region

Findings: The Middle East and North Africa demonstrate the greatest spread in happiness scores. Sub-Saharan Africa shows wider distribution with lower general levels. Latin American countries show relatively high happiness despite lower economic development.

5. Key Findings and Interpretation

  • GDP per capita is the strongest predictor of happiness scores, consistent with Stevenson and Wolfers (2008).
  • Drivers of happiness tend to occur together – GDP, social support, healthy life expectancy, and autonomy are all strongly associated with high happiness scores (Helliwell et al., 2019).
  • Regional rankings are stable – The line chart shows that regional rankings remained largely unchanged across 2015–2019, suggesting structural factors drive happiness.
  • Corruption perception requires careful interpretation – The positive correlation (r=0.40) reflects the WHR encoding (higher values = less corruption), consistent with literature on institutional trust and well-being.
  • Regional averages don't capture full experiences – Violin plots reveal significant within-region variation, highlighting the need for country-specific analysis.

6. Limitations and Recommendations

Key Limitations:
  • Self-reported well-being may be affected by cultural differences and personal interpretations (Diener et al., 1999).
  • Countries affected by conflict or political instability may be underrepresented.
  • Variables represent contribution scores, not raw measurements—associations, not causality.
  • A longer time period would allow for investigation of more recent events (e.g., COVID-19).

Recommendations: Incorporate governance indicators for deeper institutional analysis, use raw GDP data for alternative comparisons, and extend the time series to include post-2019 data.
